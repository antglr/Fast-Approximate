Fast-Approximate BCM Study

With the BCM switched off, the charge of each shot can be inferred from that shot's own laser and screen measurements to 1–2.5% at 5–10 nC and about 13 pC at 0.1 nC. What is left is the BCM's own noise and a slow calibration drift. Forecasting from the past recovers only the trend.

What was done

The work went in four stages. Each stage answered a question that decided the next one.

Stage 1 · 00–04

Understand the data

HDF5 chunks of 100 shots: scalars (BCM, laser power meters, magnets, temperatures, humidity), RF waveforms, laser scope trace (2002 samples at 25 ps, a ~50-pulse train at ~1.5 GHz), BTV0125 screen images (1216 × 1936, 12-bit).

Stage 2 · 05–07

Correlate and align

Which instruments follow the charge, shot to shot and over time, and in which row each one is stored. Per-day feature selection (|r| ≥ 0.25 with BCM, ≤ 0.5 between features).

Stage 3 · 08–08_06

Virtual BCM

Infer y(t) from the same shot's x(t), as if the BCM were lost after 70% of each segment. Trend + shot-to-shot two-stage models, losses, physics constraints, ensembles.

Stage 4 · 09–10_01

Forecast from the past

How much of x(t) is in x(t−N) (t-SNE, autocorrelation), then forecast y(t) from data up to t−N, against an oracle-trend floor.

What the data showed

  • The laser drives the charge. Shot to shot, the lab laser power meter follows BCM_GUN at r = 0.83 and the screen intensity at r = 0.79 (run 07-46-07).
  • Instruments are not stored in the same row. BCM and screen are aligned; the laser scope, the power meters and the RF waveforms are stored one row late. Every model shifts them back by one row.
  • The one-row shift is not exact for every shot. In segments 1, 5 and 7 the laser one row earlier still correlates with the BCM almost as strongly (0.37 vs 0.47, 0.38 vs 0.39, 0.67 vs 0.63).
  • The laser scope repeats itself. 23% of its traces are copies of the previous shot. They are flagged and dropped.
  • The screen needed care. Clusters of hot pixels faked peaks in 27 shots, and the faint halo makes the RMS size depend on the cut (1.7 mm with no cut, 0.28 mm at 5% of the peak).
  • Klystron, magnets and environment do not explain the charge shot to shot. Temperature and humidity only follow it over tens of minutes.
  • Each run has its own operating point. On a t-SNE map the runs form separate islands, so a model has to be fitted per segment.

What made the difference

  • Splitting trend and shot-to-shot. Each stage gets its own target and its own model.
  • The concordance loss (CCC) for the shot-to-shot stage. MSE shrinks the predicted fluctuations to 55–65% of their real size; CCC brings them to about 90%.
  • Training the trend stage on recent shots only. This took segment 1 from 196 to 121 pC by removing a +0.5 nC overshoot.
  • The physics constraint. Without it the model predicted less charge for more laser power on 40–98% of shots. With it this never happens, and the segment where the model has to extrapolate improves (230 → 214 pC).

What did not help

  • A second model trained to predict the difference (residual correction): worse, even on out-of-fold differences.
  • One model pooled over all segments: much worse. The runs behave differently.
  • Max-error loss: worst on almost every metric, max error included.
  • Longer memory for the trend (rolling means, TCN), wider networks, GRU over past shots: mixed at best.
  • Tuned ensembles: chosen on validation, neutral on test.

Most of the inference comes from the screen intensity (BTV0125). A BTV screen intercepts the beam, so check whether the screen can stay in during operation before relying on this model. A laser-and-RF-only version has not been tested yet.

Forecasting from the past

Everything is known up to shot t−N; the task is to forecast the charge of shot t. The past carries the slow drift of the state (laser power keeps r ≈ 0.5 out to 50 shots) but almost none of its shot-to-shot part (r ≈ 0 after 1–2 shots). So the best possible forecast knows the trend exactly. Its error, the oracle-trend floor, is the BCM's own shot-to-shot spread.

Two-step model x(t−N) → x̂(t) → y(t)

Step 2 works: given the true x(t) it matches the virtual BCM. Step 1 is the limit: x̂(t) recovers at best R² ≈ 0.35 (laser power), and about 0 for the spot shape and RF. The forecast is no better than the 51-shot BCM mean, at any model size.

Where forecasting stands

Up to N ≈ 20 the forecasts sit within 1–7% of the floor in every segment. At N = 50–100 the remaining error is trend drift that the past does not announce (about 170 pC of slow error in segments 1 and 5 at N = 100). The features add little beyond the BCM history and overfit in segment 3.

Recommendations and open points

  • Freeze the virtual BCM at C with the physics constraint. Model choice no longer changes the result beyond the noise of a single test.
  • Re-extract the datasets with per-shot timestamp alignment (scripts/pulses.py) on the cluster. The leftover laser timing jitter sits exactly in the segments with the largest errors (1, 5, 7).
  • Make the evaluation robust: several cut times per segment, and train on earlier runs, test on a later one.
  • Add an uncertainty band to each inferred shot and a flag when the inputs leave the training range.
  • Test a version without the screen (laser and RF only).
  • For forecasting, use the simplest model near the floor: an EWMA or the BCM-history MLP, plus a band that widens with N.

Limits of these numbers: one 70% cut per segment, so differences of a few percent are within test noise. The data in data/ was replaced once during the study (run 11-48-33 → 07-46-07); everything here uses the later data and the team's stitched segments.
